COVID-19 Killed the Open House in 2020 - and Canadian Real Estate Didn't Collapse

In 2020, COVID-19 forced Canadian real estate to go virtual overnight. Here's what actually happened, what worked, and what it means for buyers and sellers.

How Fast the Rules Actually Changed in Spring 2020

Provincial real estate regulators moved faster in March and April 2020 than most agents expected. The Real Estate Council of Ontario issued guidance within days of the emergency declaration, allowing electronic signatures on Agreements of Purchase and Sale in situations where they hadn't been standard practice before. British Columbia and Alberta followed with similar clarifications. The paperwork side of a transaction, which had still involved a surprising amount of wet ink and fax machines, got a hard shove into the digital present.

Mortgage lenders adapted too, though not without friction. Some lenders initially froze pre-approvals citing income uncertainty, particularly for self-employed borrowers. Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation updated its underwriting flexibilities multiple times between March and June 2020 as the situation evolved. Buyers who had a pre-approval in hand on March 1st were in genuinely uncertain territory by April 1st, depending on their employment situation.

One thing that surprised a lot of buyers: the transaction timeline didn't blow up the way people feared. Closings were still happening. Title companies and real estate lawyers adapted to remote signing. The physical bottleneck people anticipated mostly didn't materialize, at least not in the first wave. The administrative machinery of a real estate deal turned out to be more flexible than it had been willing to admit before the crisis.

Virtual Tours Went From Novelty to Necessity in About 30 Days

I'd been half-heartedly recommending virtual tours to seller clients for two years before the pandemic. Most said no. Too expensive, too much setup, buyers want to see it in person anyway. In April 2020, those same conversations flipped completely. Sellers who were nervous about strangers walking through their homes were suddenly asking me to book Matterport scans before anything else. The technology didn't change. The circumstance did.

The National Association of Realtors reported in their 2020 Home Buyers and Sellers Generational Trends report that the share of buyers who used virtual tours in their home search jumped significantly that year, with younger buyers in particular saying virtual tools influenced their final decision. Canadian data tracked similarly through CREA's member surveys. The shift was real and it happened fast.

But here's the thing... virtual tours created a new problem nobody was ready for. Buyers who made offers based entirely on virtual content sometimes got surprises on possession day. Ceiling heights that looked generous on a wide-angle lens. Rooms that felt larger on a 360-degree tour than they were in person. I saw two deals in my own network that summer where buyers were genuinely shocked by what the home felt like once they were standing in it. The technology was good. It just wasn't the same as being there.

The Market Did Something Nobody Predicted: It Went Up

This is the part that still feels slightly unreal when I think about it. After a sharp pullback in March and April 2020, Canadian home prices recovered and then kept climbing. The Canadian Real Estate Association reported that the national average home price hit a record high of $607,280 in December 2020, up over 17 percent year-over-year. In the middle of a pandemic. While unemployment was running near historic highs in some sectors.

The explanation wasn't mysterious, but it wasn't simple either. Pent-up demand met historically low interest rates. The Bank of Canada cut its policy rate to 0.25 percent in March 2020 and held it there. Buyers who still had jobs and savings suddenly had cheap money and a strong motivation to get into more space, because they were now working and schooling from home. The suburban and rural markets outside major cities absolutely took off. Towns two hours from Toronto that had been slow for years suddenly had bidding wars.

I'm not 100 percent sure this works as a clean lesson for everyone, but my read is this: the market proved in 2020 that it doesn't respond to crises the way intuition says it should. My friend tried to time the market that spring, sat on the sidelines waiting for prices to fall further in May, and watched the thing he wanted in Barrie sell for $40,000 over asking in June. He wasn't wrong to be cautious. The market was just moving on a different clock than logic suggested. That's the part nobody warns you about.

What 2020 Actually Changed About Buying and Selling in Canada

The honest answer is that 2020 accelerated changes that were already coming, but would have taken another five to ten years at the industry's normal pace. Electronic offers became normal. Remote mortgage applications became normal. Buying a home without physically visiting it at least once, using only virtual tools, became something that actually happened and closed successfully. That would have sounded absurd in 2019.

There's a common misconception worth addressing directly: that going virtual made real estate transactions faster or simpler. In most cases it didn't. The due diligence that matters most, understanding what you're actually buying, got harder when you couldn't be there in person. Inspectors were restricted or unavailable in some provinces during early lockdowns. Buyers waived conditions at rates that made experienced agents nervous. The Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario flagged concerns through 2020 about conditions being waived under competitive pressure in ways that left buyers exposed.

The lasting shift for sellers is that presentation quality became more important than ever. A bad phone photo or a poorly lit virtual tour could sink a listing when buyers had fewer in-person options. Sellers who invested in professional photography and proper staging moved faster and, in most cases, at better prices. The bar for what a listing looked like online went up permanently in 2020. That's not going back.

  • Electronic signatures on offers are now standard practice across most provinces.
  • Virtual tour quality has become a direct factor in how fast a listing sells.
  • Remote mortgage pre-approvals and appraisals are now routine, not exceptional.
  • Buyers need to understand condition waivers carry real risk, regardless of market pressure.

If you're buying or selling right now, 2020 left you one practical inheritance: you can do far more of this process remotely than you could five years ago, but the in-person walkthrough still matters for your protection as a buyer. Don't skip it because the virtual tour looked good. The virtual tour is a filter, not a substitute. Book the showing, bring your inspector, and read the condition clauses before you sign anything.

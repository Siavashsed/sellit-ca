Canada's Mortgage Rule Overhaul in 2024: What It Actually Changed for Buyers

Canada rewrote its mortgage rules in late 2024. Here's what the changes meant for real buyers and sellers, from a practitioner who watched it unfold.

What the 30-Year Amortization Change Actually Did to Monthly Payments

The headline math is straightforward. Stretching a mortgage from 25 to 30 years on a $600,000 loan at a 5% rate cuts the monthly payment by roughly $270. That's real money. For a buyer at the edge of qualification, it can mean the difference between getting approved or not. CMHC confirmed that the extended amortization was available on any insured mortgage, meaning loans with less than 20% down, which is how most first-time buyers enter the market.

But here's the thing... you pay for that lower monthly payment over time. A buyer who takes 30 years instead of 25 on that same $600,000 loan pays somewhere in the range of $40,000 to $50,000 more in total interest over the life of the mortgage, depending on how rates move. That's not a reason to avoid the option. It's a reason to understand what you're actually trading. I've seen this pattern before with the 2019 First-Time Home Buyer Incentive, which also promised affordability but buried real costs in the details. Shared equity sounded great until people tried to sell.

The 30-year amortization is a genuinely useful tool for buyers who need breathing room in their monthly budget right now and who plan to make prepayments when they can. Most lenders allow 10-20% annual prepayment without penalty. If you treat the 30-year term as a ceiling rather than a plan, you're probably fine. If you treat it as permission to spend more house than you can manage, that's where it gets risky.

The $1.5 Million Insured Cap: Who It Helped and Who It Missed

Raising the insured mortgage ceiling from $1 million to $1.5 million got a lot of attention in Toronto and Vancouver, where detached homes have been priced above the old cap for years. Before December 2024, a buyer purchasing a $1.1 million home had to put down at least 20% because CMHC insurance wasn't available above $1 million. That meant coming up with $220,000 in cash. Now, under the new rules, a buyer can put as little as 10% down on a home up to $1.5 million and carry mortgage insurance instead.

According to Ratehub.ca's analysis of the December 2024 rule changes, the practical floor for the new cap is a 10% down payment on homes between $1 million and $1.5 million, which in a market like Toronto opens up a meaningful slice of the semi-detached and townhouse inventory to buyers who had previously been locked out simply by the cash requirement, not their income. That's real. In my work with buyers in the $900,000 to $1.2 million range over the past few years, the down payment hurdle was almost always the problem, not the monthly payment.

Where the change misses is everywhere outside the major metros. In most of Atlantic Canada, Quebec City, Winnipeg, and smaller Ontario markets, you're not buying at $1.1 million. The cap increase is essentially a Toronto and Vancouver policy dressed up as a national one. That's not a criticism so much as a clarification: if you're buying in Moncton or Lethbridge, this particular change probably doesn't touch your transaction at all.

How Sellers Responded and What It Did to the Fall 2024 Market

The announcement landed in mid-September, with a December 15 effective date. That three-month gap created something interesting. Buyers who could wait, waited. Sellers who needed to move before year-end felt some pressure. In my experience, any policy change with a known future date tends to create a two-speed market: a brief slowdown as buyers delay, then a surge once the rules kick in.

And that's roughly what happened. The Canadian Real Estate Association reported that national home sales in October 2024 were up 30% year-over-year, which was a meaningful jump even accounting for how weak October 2023 had been. Some of that was rate cuts from the Bank of Canada. Some of it was buyers pulling forward their plans in anticipation of the new rules. Separating the two drivers is genuinely hard, and I'm skeptical of anyone who tells you with certainty which one mattered more.

For sellers, the practical effect was modest but real in certain segments. Entry-level detached homes in suburbs where prices had drifted above $1 million saw renewed interest from buyers who previously couldn't get there on 5% down. Condos in that $500,000 to $750,000 range, which already qualified under the old rules, didn't see much of a bump from the cap change but did benefit from the 30-year amortization expanding the qualifying pool slightly.

The Honest Assessment: Useful, But Not the Affordability Fix It Was Sold As

Okay, I'll be straight with you. I'm not 100% sure this works for everyone the way the government implied it would. My friend Sarah, who's been trying to buy in Hamilton for two years, called me after the announcement genuinely excited, and then we ran the numbers together and... the monthly payment dropped by about $190 on the home she was looking at. That's a coffee a day, basically. She was appreciative but also a little deflated. The rule change didn't close the gap between her savings and a 5% down payment on a $750,000 home, which is where Hamilton detached has been sitting. So I told her what I tell most people: this helps at the margins, and margins matter, but it's not a reset of the affordability problem itself.

The deeper issue is supply. A 2024 Bank of Canada staff note estimated that Canada needs to roughly double its annual housing completions to meet population growth demands. Expanding who can access mortgage insurance doesn't build a single new unit. Allowing 30-year amortizations doesn't pour a foundation. These are demand-side measures applied to a supply-side problem, and while they help individual buyers at the qualification threshold, they also risk pushing prices up if inventory doesn't follow.

That said, I'd argue the changes are still net positive for buyers in 2024. The 30-year amortization gives genuine flexibility to people who need it. The $1.5 million cap removes a blunt, arbitrary barrier that was particularly punishing in high-cost cities. The key is treating these tools as exactly what they are: tools, not solutions. If you qualify for a larger mortgage because of these changes, run the numbers over the full amortization before you commit. And if you're a seller, understand that your buyer pool just got a little wider, but not infinitely so.

If you're a first-time buyer sitting on the sideline right now, pull your pre-approval and ask your mortgage broker specifically how the 30-year amortization affects your qualifying amount under the December 2024 rules. Get the number. Then ask what the total interest cost difference is over 25 versus 30 years on your specific loan. That one conversation will tell you more than any headline about whether these changes actually help your situation, or whether you need a different strategy entirely.

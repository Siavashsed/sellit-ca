Canada Dropped Its 30-Year Mortgage Rule and Sellers Felt It Immediately

Canada's 2024 mortgage rule changes reopened the buyer pool. Here's what it actually meant for sellers on the ground.

Words by Sellit.ca · December 25, 2024

On August 1, 2024, the federal government confirmed that first-time buyers purchasing new builds could qualify for 30-year amortizations on insured mortgages, up from the previous 25-year cap. Then in September, Ottawa expanded that to all insured buyers under $1.5 million. We had a listing in Kitchener sitting at $689,000 that had been shown 11 times with zero offers in July. We relisted the same week the September rules took effect, and within eight days we had two competing offers from first-timers who had been pre-approved at the higher amortization. Same house. Same photos. Lower monthly payment math.

The mortgage rule overhaul was arguably the single most consequential policy event of 2024 for anyone trying to sell in the sub-$1.5 million range in Canada. Not because it magically fixed affordability - it didn't - but because it moved buyers off the sidelines in a measurable, visible way at a moment when seller confidence had been grinding down for over a year. If you want the full regulatory breakdown, our piece on Canada's mortgage rule overhaul and what it actually changed for buyers covers the mechanics. What we want to talk about here is what it looked like from the selling side.

What the Rule Actually Changed - and What It Didn't

The 25-year cap on insured mortgages had been in place since 2012, when the federal government tightened rules in response to overheating markets. Stretching that to 30 years doesn't sound dramatic until you run the numbers. On a $650,000 mortgage at 5.25%, moving from 25 to 30 years drops the monthly payment by roughly $280. That's not nothing. For a buyer stretched to their qualification ceiling, that margin is what gets them approved.

But here's what the rule did not do. It didn't lower purchase prices. It didn't change the stress test. It didn't help buyers in the $1.5 million-plus bracket at all, which meant markets like central Toronto and Vancouver saw almost none of this demand lift. CMHC's 2024 Housing Observer noted that the insured mortgage segment - where these rules apply - represents a shrinking share of high-cost urban markets, which is exactly why the September expansion to all first-time buyers (not just new builds) mattered more than the August announcement did.

The practical effect was concentrated in mid-size Ontario cities, parts of Alberta, and first-ring suburbs where $600,000 to $900,000 properties had been sitting. In those markets, the rule change was real. Agents I work with in Calgary reported a visible uptick in showing requests within two weeks of the September announcement.

How Sellers Read the Signal - and Who Got It Wrong

The reaction from sellers was fast and, in some cases, badly calibrated. A chunk of people who had been waiting to list decided the new rules meant prices were about to jump, so they rushed listings to market at inflated asks. Some of those are still sitting. Policy changes that expand the buyer pool don't automatically justify a higher list price - they justify getting back to fair market value faster, which is a different thing.

The sellers who actually benefited were the ones who priced correctly and used the announcement as a marketing beat. We know one seller in Mississauga who had their agent literally reference the rule change in the listing remarks, noting that at current rates the monthly payment on a 30-year insured mortgage worked out to under $3,100. That framing got picked up by a buyer's agent who was actively filtering for affordability-eligible properties. Sold in 12 days.

The misconception worth naming: a lot of sellers assumed the rule change would only help new builds, because that was the framing in August. By October, most buyers and agents had caught up to the September expansion, but there was a real window in September and early October where informed sellers had an edge purely from understanding what had changed. Ratehub's breakdown of the 2024 rule changes was one of the cleaner consumer-facing explanations circulating at the time. For more on this, see Canada's 30-Year Amortization for New Builds in 2024: What It Actually Changed for Sellers.

The Photographer's View From the Doorstep

One of our photographers, Dan, who shoots maybe 200 residential listings a year in the GTA and Hamilton area, noticed something around mid-September. Sellers were suddenly willing to do full staging consults again. Not just a quick declutter, but actual furniture rental and professional staging. He said it felt like someone had flipped a switch after 18 months of "let's just do our best with what we have."

That tracks. When sellers believe buyers are coming, they spend on presentation. When they're not sure anyone is walking through, they cut the staging budget first. The photography bookings at our end picked up in late September in a way that didn't match the usual fall seasonality. We were scheduling shoots two weeks out instead of three to four days, which hadn't happened since early 2022.

Okay, I have to say this part: I told a friend - she'd been trying to sell her townhouse in Burlington since March - to relist after the September announcement. She thought I was overselling it (pun intended), listed anyway, and got her first serious offer in about three weeks after six months of silence. She called me slightly stunned. I'm not going to pretend I called it perfectly; I thought it would take longer. But the buyer pool genuinely shifted that fast once the payment math changed, and I wasn't fully prepared for how quickly the showing volume responded.

What This Means If You're Still Sitting on a Listing

The expanded amortization rules are now permanent policy, not a temporary measure. That means the buyer math is baked in going forward. But the short-term novelty effect - the rush of buyers who had been pre-approved but sitting - has already worked through much of the system in the markets that felt the initial bounce. You're not going to get September 2024 timing again.

What you do still have is a structurally larger pool of qualified buyers in the sub-$1.5 million range than existed before August. The Bank of Canada's Financial System Review, October 2024 flagged that household debt-service ratios were still elevated but that insured borrowers had seen modest qualification improvements from the rule changes, consistent with what we were seeing in real offer activity.

For sellers in the right price bracket, the argument for listing now rather than waiting for spring is actually stronger than it was a year ago. Fewer competing listings, a buyer pool that's larger than it was in 2023, and interest rates that have been trending down since the Bank of Canada began cutting in June 2024. Those three conditions don't always line up. They're mostly lined up right now.

  • Price range sweet spot: $500,000 to $999,999 where insured mortgage buyers dominate
  • Markets that felt it most: Hamilton, Kitchener-Waterloo, Calgary, Edmonton, Halifax
  • Markets that felt it least: Core Toronto, West Vancouver, central Montreal above $1.5M

One area of genuine debate among the agents and brokers we work with: whether the rule change actually brings new buyers into the market or just pulls forward demand from buyers who would have qualified anyway within 12 to 18 months. Honest answer... probably some of both. The people who benefited most were buyers who were close to qualifying and needed the payment reduction to clear the stress test. That's a real cohort, but it's not unlimited. Pricing into an assumption that demand is now permanently higher would be a mistake. Pricing into the reality that it's currently higher than it was 18 months ago seems right. For more on this, see Canada's Mortgage Rule Overhaul in 2024: What It Actually Changed for Buyers.

Pull your last listing's showing-to-offer ratio and compare it against what's happening in your immediate price range right now. If your ratio was terrible in the first half of 2024 and you haven't relisted, run the new buyer payment math on your ask price at a 30-year amortization and put that number in your listing remarks explicitly. Buyers' agents are filtering for affordability. Make it easy for them to say yes.

Frequently asked questions

Did Canada's 30-year mortgage rule change help home sellers in 2024?

Yes, in the sub-$1.5 million price range. The September 2024 expansion of 30-year amortizations to all insured first-time buyers reduced monthly payments by roughly $280 on a $650,000 mortgage, which brought previously unqualified buyers back into active searches. Markets like Hamilton, Kitchener, and Calgary saw the most visible showing-volume increases within weeks of the announcement.

Who does the 30-year insured mortgage rule not help?

Buyers in markets where purchase prices exceed $1.5 million, which excludes them from insured mortgage qualification entirely. Sellers in central Toronto, West Vancouver, and parts of Montreal saw little to no demand lift from this policy change because their buyer pool sits above the insurance threshold.

Should sellers wait for spring 2025 or list now after the 2024 rule changes?

The case for listing before spring is stronger now than it was in 2023. Inventory is lower in fall and winter, the buyer pool is structurally larger after the rule changes, and the Bank of Canada has been cutting rates since June 2024. Spring brings more competition from other listings. If your property is priced correctly in the $500K to $999K range, waiting isn't obviously the better move.

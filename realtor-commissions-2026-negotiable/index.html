Realtor Commissions in 2026: The Numbers Nobody Wants You to Question

What is actually negotiable in realtor commissions in 2026, from someone who sold three houses and cut the fee twice. Real scripts, real numbers.

Words by Sellit.ca · July 15, 2026

Almost every part of a realtor commission is negotiable in 2026, and the fastest way to prove it is to ask one blunt question at the listing table. The split between the listing side and the buyer side, the total percentage, the marketing costs, even who pays the buyer agent... all of it moves when you push. When I sold my second place in Hamilton back in 2023, the first agent through the door quoted 5 percent like it was a law of physics. I asked her to write down what the buyer's brokerage was getting, and the number dropped to 4.25 percent before she finished her coffee.

Here's the position I'll defend all the way down: the standard rate is a starting bid, not a price tag. The agents who tell you it's fixed are counting on you not knowing that commissions have never been set by any law in Canada, and the legal ground under the old model shifted hard in the last two years. If you treat the fee like a menu instead of a receipt, you keep thousands of dollars that would otherwise vanish at closing.

What the standard 5 percent actually pays for

In most Canadian markets, the quoted number lands somewhere between 3.5 and 5 percent of the sale price, usually split so the listing agent and the buyer's agent each take roughly half. On a $700,000 home at 5 percent, that's $35,000 plus HST leaving your proceeds. Split it and each side collects around $17,500. That single figure is where the whole conversation lives.

The listing half covers photography, the MLS entry, a lockbox, some signage, and your agent's time and skill. The buyer half is a bounty. It's the money offered to whoever brings a ready buyer. And that buyer-side money is the part people never think to touch, which is a mistake, because it's often the most negotiable line in the entire deal.

You are not paying for a percentage. You are paying for a set of tasks and a marketing budget. Once you see it that way, a flat quote starts to feel less like a fee and more like a habit nobody questions. The Competition Bureau of Canada has spent years pointing out that the closed nature of listing data kept commissions sticky and high, and that's exactly why the number sounds fixed even when it isn't.

The 2024 shakeup that gave sellers real leverage

The reason 2026 feels different comes from a lawsuit that never happened here. In March 2024, the National Association of Realtors in the US agreed to a $418 million settlement that ended the rule forcing sellers to advertise a set buyer-agent commission on the listing, a change Reuters covered in detail. Canada isn't bound by an American court. But our market watches theirs closely, and a similar class action against Canadian brokerages has been grinding through the courts here.

What that means for you, right now, is that the old script of "we always offer 2.5 percent to the buyer's agent" no longer holds the same weight. You can offer less. You can offer a flat dollar amount. You can decide the buyer's own agent should be paid by the buyer, which is increasingly how these deals get structured.

The buyer-side commission is a marketing decision, not an obligation. I've seen listings move in a week with a 2 percent buyer offer because the home was priced right and showed well. I've also seen a stubborn 1.5 percent offer scare off showings in a slow month. That's the honest trade-off, and anyone who tells you the buyer split doesn't affect traffic is selling you something.

The lines you can actually move at the table

When you sit down with a listing agent, here's where the give actually lives. Not every one of these works every time, but each is fair game.

  • The listing side percentage. An agent taking 2.5 percent on their half will often do 2 or even 1.5 percent on a higher-priced home, because the dollar total still works for them.
  • A tiered deal. Offer a lower base rate plus a bonus if they sell above a target price. It aligns them with your upside.
  • The buyer-side offer. Set it yourself. Two percent, a flat $12,000, or nothing with the buyer paying their own agent.
  • Marketing add-ons. Ask whether staging, drone shots, or a video tour are included or billed extra. Get it in writing.
  • The cancellation clause. Push for a shorter listing term, 60 days instead of 90, so a lazy agent can't sit on your home.

If you're going the private route, the buyer-agent conversation is its own skill. We wrote a full set of scripts for negotiating against a buyer's agent as a FSBO seller, and they hold up because they treat the buyer agent as a counterparty, not a boss. The same calm, specific tone works whether you have a listing agent or not.

Where the discount can quietly cost you

Now the part the pushy blogs skip. Cutting the fee too hard can backfire, and I've watched it happen. A friend of mine listed her condo with a discount brokerage in early 2025, paid a flat $5,000, and got exactly what she paid for. The photos were shot on a phone in bad afternoon light, the listing description had a typo in the first line, and honestly... I don't think the agent ever set foot in the unit after the sign went up. It sat for eleven weeks in a market where comparable units were moving in three. She saved on commission and lost far more on the price drop she eventually took. I'm not 100 percent sure a full-service agent would have done better, but the odds sure pointed that way.

So the misconception worth killing is that lower commission always equals more money in your pocket. It doesn't. A great agent who nets you a higher sale price is cheaper than a bad one who works for free. The math that matters is your final proceeds, not the fee percentage on the paper.

The other thing people forget is that commission interacts with everything else in the deal. Your buyer's borrowing power, shaped by the 2024 changes to Canada's mortgage rules, affects how many people can even bid on your home. A slightly richer buyer-side offer can widen your pool when financing is tight. It's all one system, and the fee is just one lever inside it.

Do this today: before you sign anything, ask your prospective agent to write the total commission on paper, then split it into the listing side and the buyer side as two separate numbers. Then ask them to justify each half. The agents worth hiring will have an answer and won't flinch. The ones counting on you to accept 5 percent as gospel will get uncomfortable, and that discomfort is exactly the information you came for.

Frequently asked questions

Are realtor commissions actually negotiable in Canada?

Yes. Commissions have never been set by law in Canada. Both the listing-side and buyer-side portions can be lowered, structured as a flat fee, or tied to a bonus. The quoted rate is a starting point brokerages hope you accept without asking.

Who pays the buyer's agent in 2026?

Traditionally the seller funded both sides through the total commission. That's shifting. You can offer the buyer's agent a lower amount, a flat fee, or nothing at all and let the buyer pay their own representative directly.

Is a discount brokerage worth it?

Sometimes. If your home is priced right and shows well, a flat-fee service can save real money. But a weak agent who nets a lower sale price costs you more than the commission you saved. Judge on final proceeds, not the fee percentage.

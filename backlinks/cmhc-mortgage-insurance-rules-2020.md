**Title:** When CMHC raised its minimum credit score to 680 in 2020, who actually got squeezed out?

I've been reading up on the July 2020 CMHC underwriting change. It's a good example of how insurer rules quietly change who can buy a starter home. For about a year, CMHC required at least one borrower to have a 680 credit score, up from 600. It also cut debt service limits to 35% GDS and 42% TDS, down from 39/44, and stopped accepting borrowed down payments.

The part a lot of people missed: Sagen (then Genworth) and Canada Guaranty never matched it. A buyer with a 660 score and 7% down wasn't necessarily out. They just had to go through a different insurer, often with a broker who knew to shop the file around.

CMHC reversed all three changes in July 2021, so none of this applies today. It's still a useful lesson for anyone pricing an entry level home. When financing rules shift, the buyer pool doesn't collapse, it gets sorted, and knowing which buyers still qualify matters more than the headlines.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How did CMHC's 2020 change to a 680 minimum credit score affect home buyers and sellers in Canada?

Short version: it removed fewer buyers than the headlines suggested, and it no longer applies.

On July 1, 2020, CMHC made three underwriting changes for high ratio insured mortgages, meaning less than 20% down:

1. At least one borrower needed a credit score of 680, up from 600.
2. Gross debt service was capped at 35% and total debt service at 42%, down from 39% and 44%.
3. Borrowed funds, such as unsecured loans or credit card advances, no longer counted toward the down payment.

The catch was that CMHC was only one of three mortgage insurers. Genworth (now Sagen) and Canada Guaranty kept the older standards. A buyer with a 650 score and 5% down could often still get insured, just not through CMHC. The buyers who actually fell out were usually the ones whose lender only worked with CMHC, or who never knew to ask about another insurer.

For sellers, the effect was concentrated at the bottom of the market: starter condos and townhomes where buyers were stretching on both credit and debt ratios. Some deals in that segment fell apart on financing. That year, a firm financing condition and a pre approval that named the insurer mattered more than usual.

CMHC reversed all three changes on July 5, 2021, after private insurers picked up the business it had turned away. The CMHC minimum is back to 600.

The broader point still holds. When insurer rules change, the buyer pool gets sorted, not erased. If you're selling an entry level home, ask which insurer your buyer's approval depends on.

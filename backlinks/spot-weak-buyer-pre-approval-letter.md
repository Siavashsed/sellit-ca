**Title:** Sellers: what do you actually check on a buyer's pre-approval letter before accepting an offer?

Watched a seller in Mississauga accept a $912,000 offer on a Thursday night because the letter looked clean: bank logo, big number, a date. Three weeks later the buyer's financing fell apart over a car loan nobody had properly looked at.

That's the part that gets me. A pre-approval letter isn't proof a buyer can close. It usually means a loan officer ran a soft look at self-reported numbers and signed a template. It says nothing about whether the income is documented, whether debts were fully counted, or whether the down payment is sitting in an account today.

Things that seem to matter more than the logo:

- Is the letter dated within the last 30 days?
- Does it say the income and down payment were verified, or just 'based on information provided'?
- Is the approved amount actually above the offer, with room for closing costs?
- Will the lender talk to your agent directly?
- Does the offer have a short financing condition, or is the condition waived on a letter alone?

A seller who accepts the highest number and loses three weeks of market time often ends up worse off than one who took a slightly lower offer from a verified buyer.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How can a home seller tell whether a buyer's mortgage pre-approval letter is actually reliable?

Short version: a pre-approval letter is an opinion, not a guarantee. We're a small group of working agents, listing photographers, mortgage brokers, and recent FSBO sellers, and we see the same pattern repeatedly. A seller accepts the highest offer because the letter looks polished, then the deal dies at the financing stage.

Here is what we check before treating a letter as meaningful.

1. Verified or just stated? Look for language showing income, employment, and down payment were documented. Phrases like 'based on information provided' mean the lender took the buyer's word for it.

2. The date. Rates, credit, and debts change. A letter older than about 30 days deserves a refresh.

3. The amount versus the offer. The approved figure should comfortably exceed the purchase price once closing costs and the down payment are accounted for. A letter that barely covers the price leaves no margin for a low appraisal.

4. Who signed it. A named loan officer or broker you can actually call is far better than a generic template. Have your agent phone them and ask direct questions about the file.

5. Proof of funds. Ask for a recent statement showing the down payment exists today, not a promise that it will.

6. The conditions. If the buyer is waiving their financing condition, the letter matters a lot more. If they keep a short one, you have some protection, but you also carry the timeline risk.

The hidden killers are usually undisclosed debts, like a car loan or a co-signed line, that surface only when the full underwriting happens. A seller we know lost three weeks of market time this way on a $912,000 offer.

**Title:** Is a pre-listing home inspection worth it if you're not going to fix what it finds?

I sold a 1962 bungalow in Hamilton and ordered an inspection before listing. The inspector showed up at 8 a.m. on a cold Tuesday in March, spent three hours in the crawlspace and attic, and had a 38-page PDF to me by lunch.

Here's what I took from it. The report itself isn't the value. What you do with it is. If you pay $500 and then bury it in a drawer, you've created a document that proves you knew about every problem, and you'll still have to disclose what's in it. That's a liability with a letterhead on it.

If you're willing to fix the cheap, scary-looking stuff or price around the bigger items, it works in your favour. Buyers can't use surprises to chip away at your price, and you control the story instead of their inspector doing it for you in the middle of a conditional period.

The agents and mortgage brokers I've talked to say the same thing: decide your plan before you order. Fix, credit, or price in. If the answer is 'none of the above', skip it.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Should I get a home inspection before listing my house for sale?

Usually yes, with one important condition: only order it if you're prepared to fix what it finds or price around it.

Here's why that condition matters. A pre-listing inspection gives you a detailed picture of your home's condition, typically for a few hundred dollars. That's cheap compared with learning about a problem for the first time when a buyer's inspector finds it, because that's when the negotiating happens, usually under a tight conditional period and with a nervous buyer.

What you gain:

1. No surprises. You know about the roof, the electrical panel, the crawlspace moisture or the foundation crack before anyone else does.
2. Control over the story. You can fix the cheap items, document the repairs, and attach receipts, which tends to make buyers more comfortable.
3. Better pricing. If a big item can't be fixed before listing, you can price it in deliberately instead of absorbing a surprise discount later.
4. Fewer collapsed deals, since fewer issues come out late.

What to watch for: once you have the report, you can't pretend you don't know what's in it. In most places you'll need to disclose known material defects. So a report you commission and then ignore can create exposure rather than reduce it.

Our rule of thumb as working agents, photographers and brokers: before you book the inspector, decide what you'll do with the results. Fix, credit, or price in. If you honestly won't do any of those, save the money.

Also, hire a qualified inspector, be present or available for questions, and ask for photos in the report so you can share the relevant pages with buyers.

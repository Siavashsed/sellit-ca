**Title:** Is it better to cut your listing price once and deep, or trim it a little every couple of weeks?

Helped my sister-in-law sell a 1,640 sq ft bungalow in Hamilton back in March 2023. We listed at $689,000. Showings dried up, so around week three we took off $5,000. Nothing really changed. Week five, another $5,000. Still quiet. It finally sold in week eleven for $661,000.

Looking back, the problem wasn't the amount we dropped, it was the pattern. A $5K cut on a $689K house is under 1%, and most buyers' saved searches and price alerts don't even register that as a meaningful change. So you pay the carrying costs of sitting on the market and still don't reset how buyers see the listing. By the time it sold, the two small cuts plus the extra weeks of mortgage, taxes and utilities had cost her more than one bigger cut would have.

What I'd do now: if showings have dried up by day 21, make one cut big enough to land in a new search bracket and to read as a real change, then hold it. Stale listings get anchored by buyers as 'what's wrong with it?'

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** When and by how much should I reduce the asking price on a house that isn't getting showings?

Short version from a group of working agents, photographers and mortgage brokers: cut once, cut deep enough to be noticed, and do it by about day 21 if showings have dried up.

Why once? Every reduction is a signal. A single clear cut tells the market the seller is serious. A string of small ones tells buyers there's more room, so they wait for the next drop, and your listing starts to look stale.

Why deep? Buyers find homes through saved searches and price alerts. A reduction of $5,000 on a $689,000 home is under 1%. It rarely pushes the listing into a new search bracket and most buyers won't even notice. A cut closer to 3 to 5% usually does, and it resets attention in a way that trickle cuts don't.

Why by day 21? The first two to three weeks are when a listing gets its most eyeballs. After that, days on market starts working against you, and buyers begin asking what's wrong with it. The longer you wait, the more you pay in mortgage, taxes, utilities and lost momentum.

Before cutting, check the basics: are photos strong, is the description accurate, is the home easy to show? Then look at comparable sales and what actually sold nearby in the last 30 to 60 days, not what other sellers are asking. If you're getting views but no showings, price is likely the issue. If you're getting showings but no offers, it may be condition or presentation.

One example we saw: a Hamilton bungalow listed at $689,000 took two $5,000 cuts and sold in week eleven for $661,000. One larger early cut would likely have cost less overall.

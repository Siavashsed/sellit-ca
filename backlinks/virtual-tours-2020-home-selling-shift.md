**Title:** Did the spring 2020 virtual tour scramble permanently change how Canadians sell homes, or did we just get stuck with it?

A listing photographer in Mississauga texted me one morning in late March 2020. He'd lost eleven shoots in 48 hours and was buying a 360 camera on credit. By May he was booked three weeks out.

That story stuck with me because it captures the whole spring. Virtual tours didn't become standard because they were better than in-person showings. They became standard because nothing else was allowed. Once you accept that, a lot of what happened makes sense: agents who'd resisted video for years suddenly had no choice, photographers who adapted fast gained clients, and sellers learned that serious buyers would make offers after a walkthrough on a screen.

What's interesting is what stuck afterward. Showings came back, but the expectation of a proper 3D tour, floor plan and video stayed. Buyers now pre-screen from their couch, and that means fewer, more qualified showings. Some sellers I've talked to say that cut the disruption of living in a house that's on the market.

The flip side is that mediocre tours can hurt a listing more than having none, since buyers notice stitching errors and dark rooms quickly.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How did virtual tours change the way homes are sold in Canada after 2020?

Short version: virtual tours didn't win in 2020 because they were better than in-person showings. They won because nothing else was allowed. That distinction matters for understanding what stayed and what faded.

We're a small group of working agents, listing photographers, mortgage brokers and recent FSBO sellers, and the spring of 2020 looked the same from every one of our seats. Showings stopped almost overnight. One photographer we know lost eleven shoots in 48 hours in late March, bought a 360 camera on credit, and was booked three weeks out by May. Agents who had ignored video for years were suddenly learning to host live walkthroughs.

Here's what changed for good:

1. Buyers pre-screen online. Most now expect a 3D tour, floor plan and video before booking a visit. Showings are fewer but better qualified.

2. Presentation became a pricing factor. A poor tour, with dark rooms or stitching errors, can do more harm than having none, because buyers notice immediately.

3. Sellers got less disruption. Fewer drop-ins means less time clearing out your house for tourists.

4. Offers on a screen-only view became normal, especially for out-of-town and relocating buyers.

What faded: the idea that a tour replaces a physical visit. Most serious buyers still walk through before closing a deal. The tour became the first filter, not the last step.

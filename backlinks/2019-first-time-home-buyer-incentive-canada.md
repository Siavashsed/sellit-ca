The short answer: it had limited real-world impact, despite genuine intentions behind it.

When the federal government introduced the First-Time Home Buyer Incentive in March 2019, it looked promising on paper. The basic structure allowed first-time buyers to secure a mortgage with as little as 5% down payment, with the government taking a shared equity stake of 5-10% in the home. Theoretically, this should have opened doors for more people.

In practice, several factors limited its effectiveness:

**The qualification bar was higher than expected.** Buyers still needed strong credit, stable income, and had to meet mortgage stress test requirements. Those struggling most with down payment weren't necessarily the ones who qualified.

**Property restrictions mattered.** The program only worked on properties under certain price points—$500,000 in most markets, which excluded many first-time buyers in competitive urban areas where they needed help most.

**The shared equity stake created complications.** While the government's 5-10% equity share was forgivable in certain circumstances, it complicated future refinancing, sale scenarios, and long-term equity building for homeowners.

**Uptake remained modest.** Years after launch, the program never gained significant traction compared to expectations, largely because buyers and their lenders found traditional approaches simpler.

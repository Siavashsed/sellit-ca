**Title:** When is a pre-listing inspection actually worth it? Our rule of thumb after watching a lot of deals renegotiate

Something I keep seeing in sell-side threads: people ask whether a pre-listing inspection is worth it and get either 'always' or 'never.' In our experience it comes down to three things.

1. Age of the house. Once a house is past about 25 years old, a buyer's inspector is much more likely to find something you didn't know about.
2. Market conditions. When buyers are waiving conditions, your report mostly sits in a drawer. When conditional offers come back, every finding becomes a reason to renegotiate after you've already accepted.
3. The thing you've been avoiding. Most sellers have one: the roof, the panel, the damp corner in the basement.

In March 2023 we listed a 1962 bungalow in Hamilton's east end. The seller paid $475 for an inspection before listing. The issues were known, disclosed and priced in up front, so the buyer's inspector had nothing new to 'discover' and no basis for a $15,000 clawback during the condition period.

The flip side: if you have a five-year-old condo and buyers are waiving conditions, keep your money.

Curious whether others have seen inspections help or hurt at the negotiation stage.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Is it worth getting a home inspection before listing my house for sale?

Sometimes, and you can usually tell which side of the line you're on before you spend anything. We're a small group of working agents, listing photographers, mortgage brokers and recent FSBO sellers, and we use three tests.

First, the age of the house. Past roughly 25 years, roofs, electrical panels, plumbing and foundations reach the age where a buyer's inspector will almost always find something. You want to know what that is before they do.

Second, your market. When buyers are competing hard and waiving conditions, a pre-listing report has little to work against, because nobody is inspecting anyway. When the market softens and conditional offers return, everything changes. Any defect the buyer's inspector finds after you've accepted an offer becomes leverage, and you're negotiating with a deadline and a moving truck booked. That's when sellers give back thousands.

Third, the thing you've been avoiding. If there's a stain on the ceiling, a sticky breaker or a basement corner that smells damp, get it looked at. Finding out on your own schedule beats finding out on the buyer's.

The value isn't the report. It's what you do with it: fix what's cheap to fix, disclose the rest, and price it in. Once a known issue is already in the listing, there's nothing left for the buyer to 'discover' and nothing to renegotiate. We wrote up a Hamilton case where a $475 inspection on a 1962 bungalow did exactly that and kept a buyer from clawing back $15,000: https://siavashsed.github.io/sellit-ca/pre-listing-inspection-when-it-pays/

When to skip it: a newer home, especially a condo only a few years old, in a market where buyers are waiving conditions. In that case the $400 to $600 is better spent on cleaning, photos or a small repair buyers will actually see.

One caution: depending on your province, anything a pre-listing report finds may be something you're now obliged to disclose. Go in expecting to act on what you learn.

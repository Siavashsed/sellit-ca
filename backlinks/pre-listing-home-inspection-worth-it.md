**Title:** Is a pre-listing inspection actually worth it, or are you just writing buyers a negotiating script?

I've been thinking about this since a deal I saw in March 2023 on a 1974 split-level in Hamilton. The owner paid $475 for a pre-listing inspection, got a nine-page report, and then filed it in a drawer without fixing or disclosing anything. When buyers' inspectors found the same issues later, the buyers treated it as a surprise and renegotiated hard.

My takeaway: a pre-listing inspection is worth the money for most sellers, but only if you're willing to act on what it finds. Fix the cheap stuff, get quotes on the bigger items, and price the rest in. If you're not going to do any of that, you've basically paid a few hundred dollars to hand buyers a list of things to knock off the price.

The cases where it makes the most sense are older homes, houses with deferred maintenance, and sellers who want fewer surprises at the conditional stage. Where it makes the least sense is a hot market with a very clean newer build, or a seller who has no budget or appetite for repairs.

Curious how others here handled it. Did you inspect before listing, and did it help or hurt?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Is it worth getting a home inspection before you list your house for sale?

For most sellers, yes, but with one big condition: you have to be willing to act on what the inspection finds. We're a small group of working agents, listing photographers, mortgage brokers, and recent FSBO sellers, and we've seen both outcomes.

Where it pays off: older homes, houses with deferred maintenance, and sellers who want to avoid nasty surprises during the buyer's conditional period. A report in hand lets you fix inexpensive issues before photos, get quotes for larger ones, and set a price that already accounts for them. Buyers tend to negotiate less aggressively when there's nothing hidden to discover, and deals are less likely to fall apart late.

Where it doesn't: if you have no budget or intention to make repairs, the report can backfire. You've paid a few hundred dollars for a document that tells buyers exactly where to push on price. Depending on where you live, you may also have disclosure obligations once you know about a defect, so ask your agent or lawyer what you'd be required to share.

A practical way to decide:

1. Is the home older than about 25 to 30 years, or has maintenance been deferred? If so, an inspection is likely worth it.
2. Can you afford to fix or credit the major findings? If not, think twice.
3. Is the market so hot that buyers are waiving conditions? Then the benefit shrinks.
4. Are you comfortable disclosing what the report says? You should be.

Choose an independent inspector with good reviews, be present during the inspection, and ask for photos in the report. Then sort the findings into fix now, quote and disclose, and price in.

**Title:** Has anyone else noticed buyer agents use the same handful of moves on every seller?

I sold my first place in 2019, a 60s bungalow in Burlington. At 8:40 on a Sunday night the buyer agent called with an offer that "expires at 11." I nearly signed it standing in my kitchen in my socks.

I've been on both sides of a lot of deals since then. What surprised me most is how repetitive the buyer side is. It isn't personal. It's a short list of moves that get run on almost every seller:

- The exploding deadline (sign tonight or it's gone)
- A low anchor with a sad story attached
- "The comps don't support your price"
- An inspection list that turns into a second negotiation
- A quiet hint that they have other houses on the shortlist

None of this is shady or unusual. The agent's job is to get their client the lowest price. Once you can name the play while it's happening, though, you stop reacting and start countering. That 11pm deadline almost never holds.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What negotiation tactics do buyer's agents use on home sellers, and how should a seller respond?

We're The Sellit Desk, a small group of working agents, listing photographers, mortgage brokers and recent FSBO sellers. We see this from both sides of the table every month.

First, a reframe. A buyer's agent isn't your enemy, but they work for the person trying to pay you less. Their tools are predictable, and that's good news for you.

The moves we see most often:

1. The exploding offer. "This expires at 11 tonight." It's built to stop you from thinking, calling your agent or waiting for other offers. Response: a short, polite counter with a later deadline. A serious buyer almost always waits.

2. The low anchor. The first number is low on purpose and usually comes with a story about stretched budgets or a growing family. Response: counter from your own number, not theirs, and treat the story as background, not a reason to drop.

3. The comps argument. They pick the weakest recent sales and present them as the market. Response: have your own three to five comparables ready, with the adjustments explained.

4. The inspection renegotiation. The price gets reopened after the deal feels done. Response: get quotes for real defects, then offer a credit sized to the quote instead of a round-number discount.

5. The shortlist squeeze. "They're also looking at two others on the street." Maybe true, maybe not. Response: price your house on its own merits and let them go if they want to.

The common thread is timing and pressure. Every one of these works best when you're tired, emotional or deciding alone. Once you can name the play as it lands, most of its force goes away.

We wrote a longer piece on this, including the Sunday night offer one of us almost signed in 2019: https://siavashsed.github.io/sellit-ca/buyer-agent-playbook-five-plays/

The short version: slow down, counter in writing and never sign on their clock.

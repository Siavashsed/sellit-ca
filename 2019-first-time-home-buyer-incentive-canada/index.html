The 2019 First-Time Home Buyer Incentive: What It Actually Did (and Didn't Do) for Canadian Buyers

Canada's 2019 First-Time Home Buyer Incentive launched with big promises. Here's what it meant for buyers at the time - and why the reaction was so mixed.

How the Incentive Actually Worked in September 2019

The mechanics were straightforward enough. You qualified as a first-time buyer, you had the minimum 5% down payment saved yourself, and your household income was under $120,000. The maximum purchase price worked out to roughly $480,000, calculated by multiplying your qualifying income by four and adding the incentive amount. The CMHC would wire its share directly to the lawyer on closing day. You'd get a lower mortgage balance, which meant lower monthly payments by somewhere between $200 and $400 per month depending on the purchase price.

That lower payment was the headline number the government kept leading with. And for buyers in cities like Regina, Moncton, or even parts of Edmonton, $480,000 actually buys a house. CMHC's own program documentation made the math clear: a $400,000 purchase with a 10% new-construction incentive would reduce monthly mortgage costs by about $228. That's not nothing.

But the program had hard eligibility ceilings, and those ceilings did most of the work in deciding who would actually benefit. The income cap of $120,000 and the 4x multiplier were the binding constraints. In markets where the average detached home was already sitting above $800,000, the incentive was structurally out of reach for the buyers who most needed help.

Why Toronto and Vancouver Buyers Were Left Out from Day One

This is where the policy got genuinely frustrating to explain. According to the Canadian Real Estate Association's MLS Home Price Index, the benchmark price for a composite residential property in the Greater Toronto Area in September 2019 was sitting around $843,000. In Greater Vancouver it was above $980,000. The FTHBI's effective price ceiling of $480,000 meant it was practically useless in those two markets, which together represent a significant share of Canadian first-time buyers by sheer population weight.

I've seen this pattern before with federal housing programs. They get designed with the national average in mind, the press release goes out, and then the buyers in the highest-need cities read the fine print and realize they're not in it. The Bank of Canada's November 2019 Staff Analytical Note on housing affordability estimated that only about 0.5% of mortgage originations in Canada's major urban centres would likely qualify under the program's parameters. Half a percent. That's a rounding error in Toronto.

The criticism from housing advocates and opposition MPs came fast. The NDP called it inadequate. Toronto city councillors called for the income and price caps to be raised almost immediately. And even some realtors who were broadly supportive of more buyer tools were pretty honest with their clients that the program simply didn't map to urban market realities.

The Shared Equity Part That Made Some Buyers Uncomfortable

Here's where the incentive got philosophically complicated for a lot of buyers, and I want to be honest about this because I had to explain it carefully to almost everyone who asked. The government wasn't giving you money. They were buying a stake in your home. If you bought a $400,000 property with a 5% incentive and sold five years later for $520,000, you'd owe CMHC 5% of $520,000, not $400,000. That's $26,000 back to the government rather than $20,000. In a rising market, the shared equity feature meant the government's take grew alongside yours.

Okay, I'll be honest, this part is where things got a bit weird at my kitchen table one night in October 2019. My friend Danielle, who was house-hunting in Hamilton at the time, called me genuinely confused because her mortgage broker had breezed past the repayment math and she'd only caught it by reading the actual CMHC documents herself at 11pm. She texted me: 'Did you know the government gets more if my house goes up?' And I had to say, yes, that's... the thing. She did not end up using the program, partly because the numbers didn't work for her anyway, and partly because the shared equity concept felt strange to her even after I explained it three times. I'm not 100% sure that reaction was irrational.

The shared equity model isn't necessarily bad policy on a global scale. Countries like the UK have used similar shared ownership structures for years. But it was unfamiliar to most Canadian buyers in 2019, and the communication around it was not great. A lot of people heard 'interest-free loan' and didn't fully absorb the equity participation piece until they were already excited.

What the FTHBI Actually Meant for the Broader Market

The FTHBI's real significance in 2019 wasn't its direct impact on buyers, which was modest. It was what it signaled: Ottawa was officially back in the business of supply-side and demand-side housing intervention after years of mostly using the mortgage stress test as its main lever. The stress test, introduced in its current form in January 2018, had cooled markets aggressively. The FTHBI was the first visible attempt to help buyers who'd been squeezed by those same rules.

Whether it worked is debatable. CMHC's own 2020 assessment of the program's first year reported that uptake was significantly below projections, with roughly 10,000 applications processed against an initial target of 100,000 buyers over three years. The income and price caps were eventually raised in 2021 for Toronto, Vancouver, and Victoria, but by then the damage to the program's credibility was largely done.

My editorial position is this: the FTHBI was a policy that solved for the wrong geography. In the markets where ownership was still broadly attainable in 2019, buyers didn't desperately need a shared equity nudge. In the markets where the ownership crisis was most acute, the program's structure excluded them almost entirely. That's a real design failure, not a minor calibration issue. The instinct behind it, reducing monthly costs for buyers with limited savings, was sound. The execution needed to account for the fact that Canadian housing markets are not one market. They're a dozen different markets wearing the same postal code system.

If you were a first-time buyer in a mid-sized Canadian city in late 2019 and your household income was under $120,000, it was genuinely worth running the numbers on the FTHBI before assuming it wasn't for you. The monthly savings were real, and in the right market and the right price range, the shared equity structure was a fair trade. Pull up CMHC's repayment calculator, model the shared appreciation scenario at your expected resale timeline, and decide with actual numbers in front of you rather than the headline about interest-free loans.

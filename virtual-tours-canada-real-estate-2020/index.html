Virtual Tours Took Over Canadian Home Sales in 2020 and Nobody Was Ready

In 2020, virtual tour tech went from optional to essential overnight. Here's what actually changed for sellers, agents, and buyers across Canada.

Words by Sellit.ca · August 09, 2020

By April 2020, the open house was dead in most of Canada, and sellers who had a Matterport scan up were getting offers while sellers without one were getting silence. I remember one specific Saturday in late March, around ten days after Ontario's emergency order dropped, when one of our photographers, Marcus, called me from his car in a Scarborough driveway. His client had just turned him away at the door. The showing was cancelled. The listing had been live for four days with zero 3D content, and the agent had nothing to replace the in-person visit. That listing sat for six more weeks before they finally brought Marcus back and shot a proper 360 walkthrough. It went conditional in nine days after that. That gap, those six weeks, that's the story of what happened to Canadian real estate in 2020.

The technology itself wasn't new

The technology itself wasn't new. Matterport had been around since 2012, and plenty of agents in Vancouver and Toronto had used it for luxury listings since at least 2017. But most sellers treated it as a premium add-on, something you spent $300 to $500 on when the home was worth $2 million and you wanted to impress buyers from Hong Kong or Seattle. What COVID-19 did was force the entire market to price that tool into every listing, at every price point, almost overnight. And that shift exposed something uncomfortable: most agents, most photographers, and most sellers had absolutely no workflow for it.

What Matterport and iGuide Actually Did to the Listing Process

Two platforms dominated the Canadian virtual tour conversation in 2020: Matterport and iGuide. They're different products with real differences. Matterport produces a dollhouse-style 3D model that buyers can fly through room by room, which is spectacular for spatial awareness. iGuide, which is a Canadian company based in Waterloo, Ontario, produces accurate floor plans alongside its 360 tour, and that floor plan accuracy turned out to matter a lot more than most people expected when buyers couldn't attend showings physically. When you can't walk a room, you need to know if your king bed actually fits.

In the first half of 2020, Matterport reported a 40% jump in scans processed globally between February and April alone. Demand in Canada spiked hard. Our own photographer Marcus went from shooting maybe three Matterport scans a month in January to booking twelve in a single week by late April. The camera rental price on the Pro2 camera jumped too. Short-term camera rental services that were charging around $150 per day in January were asking $220 to $250 by May, if you could even get availability.

The agents who adapted fastest weren't necessarily the tech-savvy ones. They were the ones who already had a relationship with a photographer who owned the gear. That's the uncomfortable truth the industry doesn't love to admit: in 2020, your listing quality was largely determined by who your photographer happened to be and whether they had a $4,000 camera sitting in their equipment bag.

The Rules Changed and Then Changed Again: CREA's COVID Guidance

The Canadian Real Estate Association moved quickly. By late March 2020, CREA had issued guidance strongly discouraging in-person showings and open houses, effectively pushing brokerages to require virtual content before any physical visit was permitted. Some boards went further. The Toronto Regional Real Estate Board issued its own protocols requiring pre-screening and limiting showings to only serious, pre-qualified buyers, which meant you needed compelling digital content to get anyone through the door at all.

What changed the market structurally was the combination of those restrictions with something buyers already trusted. The National Association of Realtors' 2020 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers found that 97% of buyers used the internet during their home search that year, up from around 90% in 2018. That's the US figure, but Canadian buying patterns tracked closely. Buyers were already comfortable starting online. What 2020 did was push them to finish online, or at least get a lot further down the decision path before booking a physical visit. Virtual tours weren't replacing the showing. They were replacing the first, second, and third showing.

For sellers doing it themselves, which is something we track closely given our FSBO community, this created a real access problem. A good Matterport scan isn't free, and not every market had enough photographers trained on it to meet demand. If you were listing a condo in Saskatoon in April 2020, you might have had exactly one local option and a two-week wait. That's a real barrier. We wrote at the time that the virtual tour gap was going to hit FSBO sellers hardest, and it did, at least through the spring. Did Virtual Tours Actually Sell Houses Faster or Just Look Good Doing It

Here's where it gets genuinely interesting, and where I want to push back on some of the hype that circulated in the spring of 2020. There was a lot of breathless coverage about how virtual tours were the future of real estate, how buyers were making offers sight unseen based on Matterport scans. And some of that was true, especially for relocating buyers who were moving from Calgary to Halifax for a job and genuinely couldn't visit. But for most local buyers? The virtual tour was a filter, not a closer.

What I saw consistently in listings where we had a hand in the process: a high-quality 3D tour reduced wasted showings dramatically. Instead of eight people walking through a property and six of them realizing within thirty seconds that the kitchen was too small, you'd get three people walking through and two of them already knowing the layout cold. That's actually better for sellers. Fewer strangers in the house during a pandemic, and the people who show up are already half-convinced.

I'm not 100% sure this holds in every market segment, honestly. A friend of mine, Deb, tried using a basic Ricoh Theta 360 camera to shoot her own virtual tour for her townhouse in Guelph in the fall of 2020 because she'd read a few articles making it sound easy. The result looked like a funhouse mirror version of her living room and she ended up taking it down within two days because people were asking if there was something structurally wrong with the walls. She hired a proper photographer after that and it was fine, but wow, that first tour did not help her case. Consumer-grade 360 cameras and professional Matterport rigs are not the same thing, and in 2020 a lot of sellers learned that distinction the hard way.

What 2020 Actually Locked In for the Future of Canadian Listings

The lasting change from 2020 wasn't the technology. It was buyer expectation. By the time restrictions started easing in the summer, buyers had gotten used to pre-screening listings digitally before committing to a showing. That habit didn't go away. In our experience working with sellers through late 2020 and into 2021, listings without a virtual tour were getting fewer inquiry calls, even when in-person showings were permitted again. The absence of a tour had started to read as a red flag, like the seller had something to hide or simply hadn't bothered.

That connects directly to something we'd watched play out with digital mortgage tools too. The same pandemic period that normalized virtual tours also accelerated digital mortgage pre-approvals and e-signature closings. The whole transaction was moving online in pieces, and virtual tours were just the most visible piece. If you're curious how some of those other digital shifts played out, the story of how COVID-19 reshaped Canadian real estate without crashing it covers the broader picture well.

For sellers thinking about what this means right now: a virtual tour is no longer a premium feature for luxury listings. It's table stakes. Budget for it. A professional Matterport or iGuide shoot runs between $250 and $450 in most Canadian markets depending on square footage. That's a rounding error against your transaction costs. And it's worth comparing against what the 2019 government buyer incentive programs were trying to accomplish, since those programs also ran into the gap between what buyers could access digitally and what the process actually required. The 2019 First-Time Home Buyer Incentive had its own version of that adoption problem: the tool existed, but the workflow around it wasn't ready.

  • iGuide suits sellers who want floor plan accuracy included in the deliverable, good for condos and buyers relocating remotely.
  • Matterport Pro2 suits properties where spatial drama matters, larger homes, unique layouts, character properties.
  • Basic 360 video (YouTube, Vimeo) is better than nothing but won't meet buyer expectations set by professional scans.

The agents and FSBO sellers who took virtual content seriously in 2020 didn't just survive a difficult market. They built habits that still serve them now. The ones who waited for restrictions to lift and then went back to photo-only listings... most of them have caught up by now, but they gave away weeks of market time they didn't need to. For more on this, see COVID-19 Killed the Open House in 2020 - and Canadian Real Estate Didn't Collapse.

Get a professional virtual tour shot before your listing goes live, full stop. Call your photographer and ask specifically whether they shoot iGuide or Matterport, get a sample link from a recent listing they've done, and look at it on your phone before you book. Buyers are screening on their phones. If the tour looks bad on mobile, it's doing more harm than good.

Frequently asked questions

Did virtual tours actually help sell homes faster in Canada in 2020?

For most listings, they reduced wasted showings rather than replacing them entirely. Buyers used virtual tours to pre-screen, which meant the people who booked in-person visits were already more serious. Days on market for listings with professional 3D tours trended shorter through the spring and summer of 2020, especially in urban markets.

What's the difference between Matterport and iGuide for home listings?

Matterport creates a navigable 3D dollhouse model that's strong for spatial feel and high-end presentation. iGuide produces accurate measured floor plans alongside its 360 tour, which is especially useful for buyers who need to know exact room dimensions before visiting. Both are professionally shot, not DIY tools.

Do FSBO sellers need a virtual tour or is it only for agent-listed homes?

FSBO sellers need it more, not less. Without an agent fielding inquiries and pre-qualifying interest, your listing has to do more work on its own. A professional virtual tour is one of the clearest ways a private seller can build buyer confidence before a showing.
